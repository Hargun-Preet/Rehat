# Rehat: the 5-minute pitch script (version 6)

Ten slides, about 700 words, 4 minutes 50 seconds at a calm pace. It matches `Rehat_pitch_deck_v6.pptx` and `data/seed/product.json`. If a number changes in one place, change it in all three.

The app's pitch mode (`/pitch`) steps through the same ten beats. Demo cut A (20 seconds) sits inside slide 5.

## 1. Title (0:00)

On a hot afternoon in Tuas, a subcontractor with twenty workers pays for sixty minutes of labour and gets fifty. He is doing exactly what the rules ask. And no insurance policy in Singapore will pay him a cent for it. We are Team Chai-Kopi, and this is Rehat. In Malay it means rest. In Hindi, raahat means relief.

## 2. The problem (0:25)

Call him Mr Tan. Fixed-price jobs, thin margins. Heat breaks, heavy rain and haze cost him about twelve thousand dollars a year in wages paid for work not done. The average is not what hurts. The swing is. Singapore had 37 days of high heat stress in 2023, 21 in 2024 and 29 last year. He cannot budget for that, and every policy he owns pays zero for it.

## 3. The rule, and the reality (0:55)

At a heat reading of 32, MOM requires ten minutes of rest every hour for heavy work. A work permit holder is paid his declared salary whether or not there is work. And it is enforced: MOM has acted against 213 employers. But heat is the smaller part. Two-thirds of the lost hours are heavy rain, and no boss can order the rain to stop.

## 4. The size of the prize (1:25)

If every crew in construction, shipyards and process plants were covered, 63 million dollars of premium a year. Small firms are 24 million of that: about 12,400 employers. We start with the 3,100 Income can reach at renewal. And work injury insurance is a line worth close to half a billion dollars. Every one percent of it that Rehat helps keep or win is about five million a year.

## 5. How Rehat works (1:50)

Rehat is one tick box on the work injury policy Mr Tan already buys from Income. Every hour we read NEA's heat, rain and haze data at his station and compare the downtime with a normal month. For every hour beyond normal we pay eight dollars per worker, before payroll. Every policy also comes with an hourly heat alert and a compliance log, which is what an MOM inspector asks to see. And we say up front: we pay on the station, not his site.

## 6. A normal month is part of the job (2:25)

Mr Tan already plans for heat and rain. This chart is a normal year, and a normal month is part of the job. He carries that, plus a 25 percent buffer. That is his deductible. But some months take more than their share, and those are the months a boss is tempted to skip the break. Those are the months Rehat pays. He still keeps a fifth of the cost, the limit is sixty hours a year, and normal resets yearly as the climate warms.

## 7. Choose your deductible (2:55)

Mr Tan chooses his deductible. On the Standard plan he pays two dollars forty-eight per worker per week. For twenty workers that is 2,575 dollars a year. The observed 26-year back-test returns about 1,565 in an average year. We hold the price at that cautious level rather than claiming the history itself returns 65 percent. In the worst year, 9,600. A bigger deductible costs less, a smaller one more. Standard pays in about half of all months, so he sees it working.

## 8. Profitable from year one (3:30)

Rehat is profitable from the first year, with expenses budgeted at the twenty percent Income's own brief sets. Across the plan, sixty-five cents of every premium dollar go back to firms, twenty to expenses, five to reinsurance, and ten is profit: about 540,000 dollars over three years. In the first-year pilot, Income retains the risk, so planned profit is fifteen percent. From year two, stop-loss cover protects the tail. The worst case is in the contract, 480 dollars per worker. In simulation it makes a profit seven years in ten. And if MOM acts against a firm, its heat payout is forfeited.

## 9. AI and data (4:00)

Where is the AI? Not in the payout. Rules decide every dollar, so anyone can check it against NEA's data. AI sits in the interface. It reads the policy documents and quotes in minutes, explains every receipt, and turns the forecast into a warning so Mr Tan moves heavy work to the morning.

## 10. The ask (4:25)

Our ask is a 500-firm pilot from the April 2027 hot season. About 1.3 million dollars of premium, an expected profit near 190,000, and a capped worst case of 4.8 million. So our question to Income is simple. Would you pilot this? And if not, what would have to be true? Rest that is paid for gets taken. Thank you.

## Delivery notes

- One speaker for slides 1 to 3, so the story has one voice. Hand over at slide 4 if you split it.
- Pause after "gets fifty" and after "no boss can order the rain to stop".
- Say the three numbers on slide 7 slowly: 2,575, then 1,565, then 9,600.
- On slide 8, say "the twenty percent Income's own brief sets" while looking at the Income judges.
- End on the question. Do not add a line after "Thank you".
- If you run long, cut the last sentence of slide 6 and the last sentence of slide 9. Never cut the ask.

## Lines not to say

- "Work must stop at 33." MOM requires rest breaks. Work continues.
- "The employer pays one-fourth of the wage."
- "We don't insure the summer."
- "Fifteen minutes at 33 is mandatory." Say "MOM's framework sets fifteen at 33" until someone has checked MOM's FAQ page.
- Any claim about what owners do that did not come from a real interview.
